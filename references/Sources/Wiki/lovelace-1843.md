# Lovelace -- Translation and Notes on the Analytical Engine (1843)

**Summary**: Ada Lovelace's 1843 translation of Menabrea's account of Babbage's Analytical Engine, together with her own Notes A-G, which contain the first published computer program and the first articulation that a computing machine could manipulate symbols, not just numbers.

**Sources**: A_Lovelace_offprints_IEEE_plus_postscript.pdf

**Last updated**: 2026-05-11

---

## Context

In 1840, Luigi Menabrea (1809-1896) attended Babbage's lectures in Turin and published a French account of the [[analytical-engine]] in the *Bibliotheque Universelle de Geneve* (No. 82, October 1842). At the suggestion of Charles Wheatstone, Ada Lovelace (1815-1852) translated the article into English. At Babbage's suggestion, she added extensive notes of her own. The composite work -- translation plus Notes A through G -- was published in *Scientific Memoirs*, vol. iii, pp. 666-731, in 1843. (source: A_Lovelace_offprints_IEEE_plus_postscript.pdf)

The source in this wiki is Daniel P. McCarthy's 2018 bibliographical study of a surviving offprint volume from the John G. Byrne Computer Science Collection at Trinity College Dublin, which contains both the "Sketch" (Lovelace's translation and notes) and Babbage's separately published "Addition."

## The "Sketch" -- Ada's translation and Notes

The published article has four distinct sections (source: A_Lovelace_offprints_IEEE_plus_postscript.pdf):

1. **Heading** -- Identifies the article as from Menabrea's original
2. **Preface** -- A bibliography of Babbage's published work on the Difference and Analytical Engines, drafted by Babbage in consultation with Ada and her husband Lord Lovelace
3. **Translation** -- Ada's English rendering of Menabrea (pp. 669-690), with occasional translator's footnotes
4. **Notes A-G** -- Ada's own extensive commentary (pp. 691-731), compiled in close collaboration with Babbage. Notes A-F are signed "A.A.L." and Note G is signed "A.L.L."

Note G, on the computation of Bernoulli numbers, contains a tabulated algorithm that is widely regarded as the first published computer program. It includes a foldout page illustrating the step-by-step computation. (source: A_Lovelace_offprints_IEEE_plus_postscript.pdf)

## The "Addition" -- Babbage's defense

The four-page "Addition" was originally the concluding section of Babbage's preface, describing his financial struggles with the British Government over the Engine's funding (1833-1842). The editor of *Scientific Memoirs* refused to publish material criticizing the Prime Minister, Sir Robert Peel. Babbage arranged separate publication in the *Philosophical Magazine* (September 1843) and had a four-page offprint printed to be inserted into copies of Ada's "Sketch." (source: A_Lovelace_offprints_IEEE_plus_postscript.pdf)

## Ada's independence

When the editorial conflict arose, Babbage proposed that Ada withdraw her translation entirely. She refused in the strongest terms, writing to him on 6 August 1843:

> "On the one point of not withdrawing the translation & Notes from the Memoir, or consenting to its separate publication, I was entirely and finally decided; as I think neither for your advantage nor my own, to do so; added to my opinion that it would under the circumstances be dishonorable and unjustifiable." (source: A_Lovelace_offprints_IEEE_plus_postscript.pdf)

On the same day she wrote to her mother: "I declared at once to Babbage, that no power should induce me to lend myself to any of his quarrels." (source: A_Lovelace_offprints_IEEE_plus_postscript.pdf)

## The key insight: from calculation to computation

The debate over Ada's intellectual contribution has a long history. Bruce Collier (1970) dismissed her as contributing "little more to the 'Notes' than trouble." But Doron Swade (2003) offered a more precise assessment:

> "Ada saw something that Babbage in some sense failed to see. In Babbage's world his engines were bound by number. He saw that the machines could do algebra in the narrow sense that they could manipulate plus and minus signs ... What Lovelace saw -- what Ada Byron saw -- was that number could represent entities other than quantity. So once you had a machine for manipulating numbers, if those numbers represented other things, letters, musical notes, then the machine could manipulate symbols of which number was one instance, according to rules." (source: A_Lovelace_offprints_IEEE_plus_postscript.pdf)

This is the fundamental transition from calculation to computation -- the recognition that a mechanical engine operating on numbers could in principle operate on *any* domain encodable as numbers. This insight anticipates [[godel-numbering]] (encoding syntax as numbers), [[turing-1936]] (the universal machine operating on encoded descriptions), and ultimately the [[transformer-architecture]] (operating on tokenized representations of language).

## The anonymous annotations

The Trinity College copy contains pencil annotations by an unknown contemporary reader. These are repeatedly dismissive of Ada's mathematical claims: "not true," "not intelligible," "All mere verbiage," "a pretty & ladylike turn." The annotator targeted Ada's algebraic statements and appears to have been a well-educated male mathematician, possibly from Babbage's Cambridge circle. The sexist tone anticipates later skepticism about Ada's abilities. (source: A_Lovelace_offprints_IEEE_plus_postscript.pdf)

## Surviving copies

McCarthy identified eight known offprints of the "Sketch," six of which are found together with the "Addition." They are held at Trinity College Dublin, the University of London Senate House Library, King's College London, the British Library, and the National Trust at Calke Abbey, with two further copies sold at Christies New York in 2005 and 2008. (source: A_Lovelace_offprints_IEEE_plus_postscript.pdf)
