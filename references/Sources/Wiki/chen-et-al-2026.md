# Does AI Already Have Human-Level Intelligence? The Evidence is Clear (Chen, Belkin, Bergen & Danks, 2026)

**Summary**: A *Nature* commentary arguing that, by reasonable and historically grounded standards — including Turing's own — artificial systems have achieved general intelligence, and that recognizing this fact is important for policy, risk assessment, and scientific understanding.

**Sources**: Does AI Already Have Human level Intelligence the Evidence is Clear Nature.pdf

**Last updated**: 2026-05-11

---

## Context

Published in *Nature* in early 2026, this commentary responds to a paradox: LLMs pass the [[turing-test]] (see [[jones-bergen-2025]]), outperform human experts in creative writing (see [[chakrabarty-et-al-2026]]), and accelerate scientific research (see [[bubeck-et-al-2025]]), yet a March 2025 survey by the Association for the Advancement of Artificial Intelligence found that 76% of leading AI researchers thought scaling current approaches was "unlikely" or "very unlikely" to yield AGI. The four authors — from philosophy, machine learning, linguistics, and cognitive science — set out to explain and resolve this disconnect (source: Does AI Already Have Human level Intelligence the Evidence is Clear Nature.pdf).

## Key arguments

### The evidence for general intelligence

The authors cite three main lines of evidence:

1. **The Turing test**: GPT-4.5 was judged human 73% of the time in a three-party Turing test — more often than actual humans ([[jones-bergen-2025]])
2. **Creative output**: Readers preferred LLM-generated literary texts over those by expert human writers ([[chakrabarty-et-al-2026]])
3. **Scientific contribution**: Frontier models accelerated real research across mathematics, physics, biology, and computer science ([[bubeck-et-al-2025]])

(source: Does AI Already Have Human level Intelligence the Evidence is Clear Nature.pdf)

### Three sources of the disconnect

The authors identify why expert opinion lags behind the evidence:

1. **Conceptual confusion**: Definitions of AGI are "ambiguous and inconsistent." Different researchers mean different things, and the term is entangled with commercial interests (e.g., OpenAI's charter ties AGI to specific contractual milestones). This makes coherent assessment difficult.
2. **Emotional resistance**: AGI "raises fear of displacement and disruption." Researchers may be motivated to deny the conclusion because its implications are threatening.
3. **Practical entanglement**: The term AGI is used in business contracts and policy discussions in ways that distort technical assessments.

(source: Does AI Already Have Human level Intelligence the Evidence is Clear Nature.pdf)

### The case for recognition

The authors argue that recognizing AI as generally intelligent does not require believing it is conscious, sentient, or morally equivalent to humans. It simply means acknowledging that "the long-standing problem of creating AGI has been solved" by Turing's own standard: machines that can pass themselves off as humans to unaware humans. They urge "eyes unclouded by dread or hype" for clear-headed policy and risk assessment (source: Does AI Already Have Human level Intelligence the Evidence is Clear Nature.pdf).

## Methodology

This is a commentary, not a research paper. It synthesizes existing empirical results and applies philosophical analysis. The four authors come from different fields (philosophy, ML, linguistics, cognitive science) and report reaching consensus "after extensive discussion" (source: Does AI Already Have Human level Intelligence the Evidence is Clear Nature.pdf).

## Limitations and counterarguments

- The commentary does not engage deeply with specific technical limitations of LLMs (e.g., hallucination, lack of persistent memory, inability to learn from experience without retraining).
- The 76% of surveyed researchers who are skeptical are not wrong by default — the commentary primarily reframes the question rather than resolving underlying technical disagreements.
- The term "generally intelligent" is doing significant work; reasonable people can disagree about whether passing the Turing test (which rewards social mimicry) constitutes general intelligence.
