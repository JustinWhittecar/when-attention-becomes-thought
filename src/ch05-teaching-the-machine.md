## Narrative job

Follow the neuron after 1945. Chapter 4 showed that computing's founding formal object was a model of a neuron, and that the imitation sits in the material rather than in each builder's motive. Here Turing makes the imitation deliberate: in 1948 he stops borrowing the unit and starts borrowing the upbringing, randomly wired machines organised by training. Two audits then name what the imitation still lacked, Kleene's (memory) and von Neumann's own in 1958 (low precision, shallow depth, statistical meaning, massive parallelism), a list every later chapter fills in. End with the reader able to see "the brain is a computer" as a design programme with a date, an object, a lineage, and a stated exam, and to see why the imitation game is that programme's terminal test rather than a philosopher's dodge.

## Reading list

The first four entries are the spine, and their reading notes are complete. The remaining entries are targeted consults, pulled in during drafting rather than read as full nights. This chapter was split from Chapter 4 on 2026-09-29, and its reading came with it.

1. Turing, A. M. (1948). "Intelligent Machinery." National Physical Laboratory report (reprinted in *Machine Intelligence 5*, 1969). Turing's unpublished sketch of "unorganised machines," randomly wired networks of logical units that could be trained. The bridge between McCulloch-Pitts and Turing 1950, and a precedent for Chapter 7's perceptron.
2. Kleene, S. C. (1956). "Representation of Events in Nerve Nets and Finite Automata." In *Automata Studies*, Shannon, C. E. & McCarthy, J. (eds.), Princeton University Press, 3-41. Formalizes what McCulloch-Pitts nets actually compute, finite automata and regular events, and is the primary source for the Turing-equivalence claim the 1943 paper only asserts.
3. von Neumann, J. (1958). *The Computer and the Brain.* Yale University Press (Silliman Lectures, published posthumously). Read whole; it is short. Von Neumann's own comparison of neural and digital computation, and a built-in counterweight: he argues the brain is not simply digital.
4. Turing, A. M. (1950). "Computing Machinery and Intelligence." *Mind*, 59, 433-460. The chapter's closer. The question "can machines think?" becomes coherent only after Chapter 1's logic and Chapter 4's neuron-and-stored-program convergence are in place.
5. Hebb, D. O. (1949). *The Organization of Behavior.* Read Chapter 4 ("The First Stage of Perception"). The learning rule McCulloch-Pitts deliberately avoided, included here so that Chapter 7 has a precedent to invoke.
6. Wiener, N. (1948). *Cybernetics: or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine.* Read the introduction. Background, cited where the chapter touches feedback and purpose.
7. Heims, S. J. (1991). *The Cybernetics Group.* MIT Press. Optional. The institutional history of the Macy Conferences, where McCulloch, Pitts, von Neumann, Wiener, and Shannon shared a room and "the brain is a computer" became a research program.
8. Lettvin, J. Y., Maturana, H. R., McCulloch, W. S. & Pitts, W. H. (1959). "What the Frog's Eye Tells the Frog's Brain." *Proceedings of the IRE*, 47(11), 1940-1951. Optional counterweight. The same authors, sixteen years later, finding the brain to be a messy feature-detector rather than a clean logic engine.

## Worked examples to build into the chapter

- Turing's own five-unit A-type machine, traced for five ticks, then one B-type connection with its interference inputs.
- A net that fires on every third input pulse, which no net without loops can do: Kleene's boundary made visible, built on the one-bit circle from Chapter 4.
